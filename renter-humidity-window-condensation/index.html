The short version: Window condensation is a humidity and temperature problem you can measure. Get a number first, cut the obvious indoor moisture (showers, drying racks, a bubbling pot with no fan), then size a dehumidifier to the room you have — not only to the square footage on the box. Wipe the glass. Use a small moisture absorber in a closet. This is not mold remediation. Hidden, spreading, or health-related mold is a landlord and professional issue, and sometimes a housing-code issue, not a shopping list.

Measure first, then size the machine

Condensation on a window can mean the indoor air is wet, or the glass is simply very cold, or both. A hygrometer on an interior wall, out of the sun and away from the kettle, gives you the indoor relative humidity. Many comfort guides talk about a mid-range indoor humidity (often roughly 30–50%, depending on the season and the person). We are not going to invent a perfect target for your climate. We are going to say: if you are consistently high, and the glass is wet every morning, you have a moisture problem worth addressing.

Dehumidifier listings often quote a large coverage area. The 25-pint hOmeLabs unit here is a mid-size home machine; the manufacturer’s square-footage claim is much larger than a typical studio or one-bedroom. In a small rental, a machine like that may be more than enough, or it may still be asked to work in a damp climate with poor ventilation. Use the tank rating, the actual humidity drop you see over a few days, and the sound and power draw you can live with. Empty the tank, or set up a drain only if you have a sink and a safe hose run that the lease allows.

A fan does not remove moisture by itself, but moving air across a cold window and out of a dead corner can reduce condensation and help a dehumidifier “see” the whole room. Run the bath fan during and after a shower. Crack a window when you cook, if the weather and the lease make that reasonable. Dry laundry in a way that does not turn the studio into a kiln, if you can.

Windows, closets, and an honest limit

Wipe visible condensation. Standing water on a sill is how paint and wood get stained, and how a small problem looks like a big one at inspection. A simple squeegee or a towel is enough; you do not need a special tool to start. Keep closets slightly open if they smell musty, and put a fragrance-free absorber in the ones that stay wet. Scented products can mask a smell you should be investigating.

Draft kits and weatherstripping can help if a leaky sash is pouring cold air over a wet room, but they will not fix a failed window or a leak from outside. If the wall or the sill is wet from the exterior, that is a building repair.

About mold: surface mildew on a wipeable sill is a maintenance task. Mold in insulation, under flooring, behind a swollen dresser, or in a pattern that keeps coming back after you dry the room, is not something to “treat” from a blog post. Tell the landlord, document it, and use local housing guidance if you need to. If anyone in the home has symptoms that concern you, that is a medical and housing question, not an affiliate question.

Will this come off clean? A dehumidifier and a cup of absorber are freestanding. Weatherstripping should be removed slowly at move-out; photograph the sash first. Do not paint over a problem, and do not caulk a window shut in a way you cannot reverse.

What not to do about condensation

  • Do not paint over mold or use a blog post as mold remediation.
  • Do not block vents or windows with furniture.
  • Do not let water sit on sills, trim, or carpet.

A reading on a hygrometer gives you something real to share with the landlord.

Move-out checklist

  • Wipe and dry every window sill.
  • Remove window film or tape slowly.
  • Empty, clean, and dry the dehumidifier tank.
  • Check closets and behind furniture for moisture or mold.
  • Report any leak or mold you could not safely clean.

How-to card

Run a one-week humidity check

A simple way to see where moisture builds up before you buy the wrong-size dehumidifier.

  • Time: 7 days
  • Cost: Under $40
  • Deposit risk: None

What you need

  • One or two hygrometers
  • A notebook or phone notes
  • A towel or squeegee
  • A fan for cooking and showers

Steps

  1. Put one hygrometer in the main room and another in the wettest room.
  2. Record readings in the morning and evening for a week.
  3. Note cooking, showers, laundry, and window condensation.
  4. Wipe windows each morning and see whether the sill stays wet.
  5. Use the readings to choose ventilation, a dehumidifier, or a maintenance request.

Renter note: Leaks, water damage, and spreading mold are landlord issues. Report them in writing with dates and photos.

Why do my windows sweat in a rental?

Indoor moisture hits cold glass. Cooking, showers, drying clothes, and a tight, poorly ventilated apartment all raise humidity. A hygrometer tells you whether the air is actually wet.

What size dehumidifier do I need in a small apartment?

Start from the humidity you measure and the room you have, not only the square-footage claim on the box, which is often generous. A mid-size pint rating can be plenty in a studio; a very large unit may be overkill and still run often in a damp climate. Watch the tank and the humidity drop for a few days.

Will a dehumidifier raise my electric bill?

It will use power, and in some threads people report a noticeable bill. How much depends on the unit, how long it runs, and your utility rate. We will not invent a number. Use the humidistat so it is not running at full blast after the room is already dry.

Are moisture absorber cups enough?

For a closet or a cabinet, they can help. For a wet apartment, they are not a substitute for ventilation and, if needed, a real dehumidifier.

Is window condensation a mold emergency?

Not by itself. Wipe it, lower the humidity, and watch the sills. Hidden or spreading mold, or a leak from the building, should go to the landlord and, if needed, a professional. This site is not a mold-remediation service.
