How to Sleep Through a Snoring Partner (Bedroom Setup, Not Medical Advice)

The short version: To sleep next to a snorer, layer your defenses: fall asleep first if you can, wear well-fitted earplugs or sleep earbuds, and run steady masking sound on your side of the bed. Separate blankets cut down on the nudging and tugging that wake you. A bigger gap between pillows helps a little. And if the snoring is loud, nightly, or comes with gasping or pauses in breathing, the snorer should see a doctor. That can be sleep apnea, and no gadget fixes it.

A thread on r/AskUK was started by someone whose wife was struggling to sleep through his snoring. He wrote that he had “tried everything that Google says.” One of the most upvoted replies did not suggest a product. It suggested a sleep study. That is the right order: the snorer checks whether it is a health problem, and meanwhile the person who is awake sets up the bedroom to sleep anyway.

First, when snoring is a health question

This is a bedroom-setup guide, not medical advice. But it would be wrong to skip this part. Snoring can be a sign of obstructive sleep apnea, where breathing stops and starts during sleep. Signs that the snorer should talk to a doctor include:

  • Very loud snoring most nights.
  • Pauses in breathing, gasping, or choking during sleep. The partner is usually the one who notices.
  • Feeling very sleepy in the day, or falling asleep while driving.
  • Morning headaches, or high blood pressure.

A doctor may suggest a sleep study. Treatment is a medical decision, so we do not recommend devices for it here.

Fall asleep first

Many partners say the single best habit is to go to bed earlier, or fall asleep before the snorer does. Snoring is often quieter early in the night and louder in deep sleep. Once you are deeply asleep, you are less likely to wake.

Protect your ears

Earplugs are the strongest tool you control.

  • Foam earplugs. Mack’s Ultra Soft are listed at 33 dB NRR. Roll them thin, pull your ear up and back, and hold them in while they expand. A good fit matters more than the rating.
  • Silicone or reusable plugs. Loop Quiet 2 are soft and reusable. Some people find them more comfortable on the pillow side.
  • Sound at your ears. Sleep earbuds or a headband speaker play masking sound right at your ears. They suit people who find silence plus snoring worse than sound plus snoring.
  • Sleep headphones. The MUSICOZY headband is one option, with flat speakers inside soft fabric, so there is no hard bud pressing on your ear when you lie on your side.

Foam earplugs are cheap enough to try several sizes and shapes. If one hurts, try another brand before you give up on earplugs.

Cover the sound

Snoring is rhythmic, which is part of why it is so hard to ignore. Steady sound blurs it. Put a LectroFan Classic or a Yogasleep Dohm on your nightstand, close to your head. Try brown noise or fan sounds, which have more low end, at a volume you can sleep through. Masking plus earplugs together often works better than either alone. For more on choosing sounds, see what to do when white noise isn’t enough.

Separate bedding, same bed

Much of the waking is not the sound. It is the tug of a shared blanket, the heat, and the nudge to roll over. Two lighter comforters instead of one big one fix that. A lightweight queen comforter or two Amazon Basics down-alternative comforters in a smaller size let each person sleep at their own temperature. This is common in many European countries. For more on sharing a bed with a hot sleeper, see cooling bedding for hot sleepers who share a bed.

Small changes in the room

  • More space. A bigger bed or a slight gap between pillows puts a little distance between you and the sound.
  • A plan for bad nights. Agree in advance that either of you can move to the couch or a spare room without it being a fight. Sleeping apart sometimes is not a relationship failure.
  • Comfort for the snorer. Some people snore more on their back. A pillow behind the back, or sleeping on their side, is a comfort change worth trying. If the snoring may be a health issue, that is for a doctor, not a pillow.
  • Avoid the obvious triggers. Many people snore more after alcohol or when congested. That is a conversation, not a product.

Talking about it without a fight

Snoring is awkward to bring up, because the snorer is asleep for the part that matters. A few things make the talk easier:

  • Pick a calm time, not 3 a.m. when you are exhausted.
  • Describe, don’t blame. “You snored most of the night and I heard a few long pauses” is more useful than “you kept me up again.”
  • Record a short clip with your phone, with their agreement. Many snorers have no idea how loud it is, and a recording can help them decide to see a doctor.
  • Make a shared plan. You handle your ears and the sound machine. They handle the doctor visit if it is needed. Agree on what happens on a bad night.
  • Check in after a week. If you are still not sleeping, change one thing at a time.

What not to do

  • Do not ignore pauses in breathing or gasping. That is a reason for the snorer to see a doctor.
  • Do not buy anti-snoring gadgets as a substitute for medical advice.
  • Do not turn a sound machine up to a level that hurts your ears.
  • Do not keep earplugs in if you are the only one who can hear a baby, an alarm, or a smoke detector, unless you have a backup like a vibrating alarm.
  • Do not use sleeping pills to get through it without talking to a doctor.

Move-out checklist

  • Snoring setups do not touch the walls, so there is little to undo.
  • If you added a door sweep or weatherstripping for a spare room, remove it carefully.
  • Wash and pack bedding.
  • Check for marks on walls behind the bed from pillows or a headboard.
  • Photograph the bedroom after cleaning.

How-to card

Set up the bedroom for sleeping next to a snorer

Ears, sound, and blankets, set up before bedtime.

  • Time: 20 minutes
  • Cost: Low
  • Deposit risk: None

What you need

  • Foam or silicone earplugs
  • A sound machine or sleep earbuds
  • Two lighter comforters
  • A plan for bad nights

Steps

  1. Agree on a bedtime that lets you fall asleep first.
  2. Put the sound machine on your nightstand, close to your head.
  3. Try brown noise or fan sounds at a volume you can sleep through.
  4. Fit earplugs or sleep earbuds before you lie down.
  5. Make the bed with two comforters instead of one.
  6. If the snoring is loud every night or you notice pauses or gasping, ask the snorer to see a doctor.

How can I sleep next to someone who snores?

Try falling asleep first, wear well-fitted earplugs or sleep earbuds, run masking sound near your head, and use separate blankets.

Do earplugs work for snoring?

They can help a lot if they fit well. Foam plugs usually block more sound; silicone plugs can be more comfortable.

Does white noise help with snoring?

Steady sound can blur rhythmic snoring. Many people prefer brown noise or fan sounds.

When is snoring a medical problem?

Loud, nightly snoring with pauses in breathing, gasping, or daytime sleepiness can be a sign of sleep apnea. The snorer should see a doctor.

Is it OK to sleep in separate rooms because of snoring?

Many couples do sometimes. Agreeing in advance makes it easier and less personal.
