Where to Put a Dog Crate in a Small Apartment: 6 Spots That Work (and 3 to Avoid)

In a 600-square-foot apartment, a 36-inch wire crate is not a dog bed. It is the biggest piece of furniture in the room after the sofa. So it ends up wherever it fits: wedged against a radiator, pushed into a dark bedroom corner, or parked in front of the only window. Then the dog cries at night, the tray scratches the floor, and a spilled water bowl soaks into a rental carpet you will be charged for at move-out. The fix is not a bigger apartment. It is choosing the spot first, then the crate that fits it, then protecting the floor underneath.

The short version: Put the crate where you spend your evenings, out of direct sun and away from heaters, radiators, drafty doors, and the main walkway, with a few inches of air around it. For a puppy or a new dog, move it into or next to your bedroom at night. Measure the spot before you buy, pick a double-door crate if it has to sit against a wall or in a corner, and put a waterproof mat under it so the floor never touches the tray. If the crate has to live in the living room, a furniture-style crate that doubles as an end table saves the most space. What a good crate spot needs (before you look at the floor plan)

A crate spot has three jobs: keep the dog comfortable, keep the dog near you, and keep the rental undamaged. The Humane World for Animals crate training guide recommends placing the crate where the family spends a lot of time, such as the family room, and moving it into your bedroom or a nearby hallway at night at first, especially for a puppy, so the dog does not link the crate with being shut away.

In a small apartment that gives you a short checklist for any spot:

  • Near people. The living area during the day, your bedroom at night if your dog is new to the crate.
  • Steady temperature. Not in direct afternoon sun, not against a radiator or baseboard heater, not in front of a heating or AC vent, and not beside a drafty door or window.
  • Out of the traffic lane. Nobody should have to step around the crate to get to the kitchen or the bathroom, and the door needs room to swing open.
  • Air on all sides. Leave a few inches between the crate and the wall so air moves and the wire does not rub the paint.
  • A floor you can protect. A hard floor with a mat, or a rug you do not mind washing. Not your landlord's wall-to-wall carpet with nothing under the tray.

Measure the spot before you shop, with the crate door open. A 30-inch crate needs about 30 inches of wall, but the door may need another 20 inches of clear floor to swing. Tape the outline on the floor with painter's tape and live with it for a day before you buy.

6 spots that work in a small apartment

1. Beside the sofa, as an end table

This is the most useful spot in a studio or one-bedroom. The dog is next to you in the evening, and the crate replaces a piece of furniture you would have bought anyway. A furniture-style crate has a solid top for a lamp or a drink. The Feandrea listing gives the inside size as 35.8 by 22.4 by 26.4 inches and says it fits medium dogs up to 70 lb, so measure your dog and the space next to the sofa. Its side door can be mounted on either side, which helps when one side is against the sofa arm.

If your dog is fully crate-trained and calm, the New Age Pet ecoFLEX crate is another end-table style. The listing is unusually direct: it says the crate is for calm, crate-trained adult dogs and is not chew-proof, not for puppies still in training, and not for dogs with separation anxiety. Take that at its word. A puppy or a chewer belongs in a wire crate.

2. In a living room corner

Corners are the easiest place to fit a wire crate, but a single front door often ends up facing the wall. A double-door wire crate solves that: one door on the end, one on the long side, so you can open whichever one faces the room. The MidWest listing says the 30-inch iCrate is for dogs about 21 to 40 lb, includes a leak-proof tray and rubber feet, and comes with a divider panel so a puppy can start in a smaller space. Keep the corner crate away from a radiator or a heating vent, which are often in exactly that corner.

3. Next to your bed at night

For a puppy or a dog new to the crate, the bedroom is often the best night spot, because you can hear when the dog needs to go out. In a small bedroom, the space beside the bed or at its foot usually works. If there is no room for a second crate, a soft collapsible crate can live in the bedroom at night and fold flat by day. Soft crates are best for calm dogs. A dog that chews or claws at fabric can get out of one, so use a wire crate for a puppy or a dog that is still learning.

4. Under a window that does not get direct sun

A north-facing window, or a window that is shaded in the afternoon, can be a good crate spot because it gives the dog something to look at. Check the spot at the hottest and coldest times of day before you commit. If the window is drafty in winter, our guide to drafty rental windows covers the renter-safe fixes, but the crate should still sit a little away from the glass.

5. Behind a room divider in a studio

In a studio, a divider or a bookcase can give the crate a quieter corner while keeping the dog in the same room. This helps dogs that bark at every hallway noise, because they cannot see the door. Our guide to no-drill room dividers for a studio covers freestanding options that do not need ceiling anchors. Leave a gap so you can still see the dog from the sofa or bed.

6. In a wide hallway (only if it is wide)

Some apartments have a hallway wide enough to hold a crate and still let two people pass. If yours does, it can be a good night spot near the bedroom. If you have to turn sideways to get by, skip it. A crate in a narrow hall is a trip hazard and can block your way out in an emergency.

Bathrooms and closets look tempting in a small apartment because they are out of the way. That is exactly the problem: the dog is shut away from you, which is what the crate is meant to avoid. A washer or dryer next to the crate is also loud and can scare a dog.

Size the crate for the dog, then check it against the room

The Humane World crate guide says the crate should be large enough for your dog to stand up and turn around in, and that a growing puppy needs a crate sized for their adult size. A divider panel lets you use the big crate while blocking off part of it for a puppy. In a small apartment, do not size up just in case. A bigger crate than your dog needs takes floor you do not have.

Measure three things before you order:

  1. Your dog. Nose to base of tail, and floor to top of head while standing.
  2. The spot. Length, depth, and height, plus the clearance for the door to swing open.
  3. The path in. The crate has to get through the front door, the hallway, and around the furniture. Folding wire crates and soft crates are easier here than one-piece furniture crates.

Make a wire crate look less like a cage

A fabric crate cover slips over a wire crate and makes it read as one dark box instead of a grid of wire. Many dogs settle better in a covered crate because it feels more like a den. A few cautions:

  • Leave at least one side, or the door panel, rolled up so air can move.
  • Do not cover a crate in direct sun or near a heater.
  • Buy the cover sized to your crate, not the one that is close enough, so it does not sag into the wire where a dog can chew it.

A flat crate top can also hold a small basket for leashes and treats, which keeps them off the counter. Keep anything the dog could pull through the bars, like cords and plants, out of reach.

Protect the floor and walls so the crate does not cost you the deposit

Crates damage rentals in three ordinary ways: the tray and feet scratch hard floors when the dog shifts, water and accidents soak into carpet, and wire edges rub paint and baseboards. All three are easy to prevent.

  • Put a waterproof mat under the crate. An under-crate mat with a rubber backing keeps the tray off the floor and catches spills. The listing describes a carpet top with a rubber backing and a PET edge that holds liquid in, and says you can hose it off. Measure your crate footprint; this one is 23.6 by 47.2 inches.
  • Line the crate floor. A washable crate liner absorbs accidents and spilled water, so they stay off the tray and the floor. Keep a spare so one can be in the wash.
  • Clean accidents with an enzyme cleaner. If anything does reach the carpet, an enzyme stain and odor cleaner breaks down urine rather than covering it. Our guide to pet urine on rental carpet walks through the full cleanup.
  • Keep the crate off the wall. Leave a few inches between the wire and the paint, and slide the crate out once a month to vacuum behind it.

If the crate is noisy or the dog cries

In an apartment, a rattling crate or a crying dog is also a neighbor problem. A mat under the crate cuts the rattle on hard floors. Most crying in the first weeks is about training, not the spot. The Humane World guide suggests building up crate time in small steps and never using the crate as punishment. It also notes that puppies under 6 months should not stay in a crate for more than three or four hours at a time, and that adult dogs should not be crated for more than 4 to 5 hours during the day. If your dog cries for long periods, panics, or tries to escape, ask your vet or a certified trainer. That can be a sign of separation anxiety, and no crate placement fixes that.

Move-out checklist for a crate in a rental

  • Fold or take apart the crate and lift it out. Do not drag it across the floor.
  • Check the floor under the crate and mat for scratches, dents, or stains.
  • Clean any marks on carpet with an enzyme cleaner and let it dry fully.
  • Wipe the wall and baseboard where the crate sat and touch up any rub marks only if your lease allows it.
  • Take dated photos of the spot once it is clean.

Set up a dog crate spot in a small apartment

Pick the spot, tape it out, protect the floor, and set up the crate so the dog stays near you.

  • Time: About 30 minutes
  • Cost: Low
  • Deposit risk: Low

What you need

  • Tape measure
  • painter's tape
  • a crate sized for your dog
  • a waterproof under-crate mat
  • a washable crate liner or bed

Steps

  1. Choose a spot in the room where you spend your evenings, away from heaters, vents, drafty doors, and direct sun.
  2. Measure the spot, including room for the crate door to swing open.
  3. Tape the crate outline on the floor and walk around it for a day to check it does not block a path.
  4. Lay a waterproof mat on the floor where the crate will sit.
  5. Set the crate on the mat with a few inches of space between the crate and the wall.
  6. Add a washable liner or bed, a water bowl that clips to the crate if your dog tips bowls, and a safe chew toy.
  7. For a puppy or a new dog, move the crate into or next to your bedroom at night for the first few weeks.

Renter note: A crate on a mat leaves no marks on a rental floor. Keep the crate a few inches off the wall so the wire does not rub the paint or baseboard.

Where should I put a dog crate in a small apartment?

In the room where you spend the most time, such as the living area, away from heaters, vents, drafty doors, and direct sun. For a puppy or a dog new to the crate, move it into or next to your bedroom at night.

Should a dog crate go in the bedroom or the living room?

Both can work. Many owners use the living room by day and the bedroom at night, especially for puppies, so they can hear when the dog needs to go out. A soft or folding crate makes it easier to have a crate in each room.

Is it okay to put a dog crate in the bathroom or a closet?

It is usually a poor choice. It isolates the dog, and bathrooms and laundry spaces are often damp, hot, or loud. A corner of the living area is better.

How do I stop a dog crate from scratching my rental floor?

Put a waterproof mat with a rubber backing under the crate, lift the crate instead of dragging it, and use a washable liner inside so spills stay off the floor.

Are furniture-style dog crates good for small apartments?

They can save space because the crate doubles as an end table. Check the size and the maker's notes. Some furniture crates are meant only for calm, crate-trained adult dogs, not puppies or chewers.
