No-Drill Ways to Hang Curtains When the Lease Says No Holes

The short version: A typical curtain rod needs two brackets and four screws. If the lease says no holes, you have three real families of workaround: a spring tension rod inside a recessed frame, adhesive rod hooks on a compatible wall, or trim brackets that tap into wood casing. None of them is universal. Tension rods need a frame they can press against. Adhesive hooks have a weight limit and can peel paint. Trim brackets avoid wall holes but leave small marks in the wood. Measure the window, read the lease, and decide which trade-off you can live with at move-out.

An Amazon Basics spring tension rod (54–90") that presses into the window opening. No screws, and a clean removal, if the frame is deep enough and square enough to hold the spring.

Best when you have wood trim

Kwik-Hang 5/8" bronze brackets or the 1" black brackets for a thicker rod. They tap into the casing instead of the wall. That is not zero-trace — it is small marks in the trim.

Best adhesive option

Command curtain-rod hooks in black or satin nickel, or a no-drill adhesive blackout panel set, used only within the stated weight and on a compatible wall.

Curtains are often the first thing a no-holes lease blocks, because the default hardware is a pair of brackets and a drill. The same constraint shows up in shift-work and dark-room threads: people need the curtains to stay up, not just look pretty on a Sunday. If your real goal is blocking light, read this together with blackout curtains that actually block light — a perfect no-drill mount still fails if the fabric leaks at the edges.

A wraparound rod can help with those edge gaps, but be honest about the hardware. The Amazon Basics 48–88" wraparound blackout rod and the 28–48" version are typically screw-mounted. They are the right shape for light leaks, not a no-holes solution, unless your lease allows wall hardware.

Tension rods, adhesive hooks, and trim brackets

A tension rod is the cleanest no-holes option when it fits. The rod sits inside the window opening and holds by spring pressure. It comes down in a minute, and it should leave the frame alone. The failures are predictable: a frame that is too shallow, too tapered, or too slick; a rod that is the wrong length for the span; or panels that are heavier than the spring can hold, so the rod slowly walks down the jamb. Measure the inside width at the top, middle, and bottom. If those numbers disagree by much, a tension rod may slip.

Adhesive curtain-rod hooks exist for the case where you want the rod outside the frame, closer to how a standard rod sits. They are still adhesive hooks. The wall has to be compatible, the rod and curtains have to stay inside the printed weight, and removal follows the same stretch-the-tab rule as other Command products. A heavy blackout pair plus a metal rod is how these hooks get overworked.

Kwik-Hang-style brackets are the option people mean when they say “no drill” but still want a rod on the trim. They tap into the top of wood casing. There is no wall hole, and there is no drywall anchor. There is also no honest way to call that zero-trace: you are leaving small marks in the trim. On painted casing, those marks may be visible at move-out. If the “trim” is actually foam, vinyl, or a thin cladding, this is the wrong product.

Will this come off clean at move-out?

Photograph the window, the trim, and the wall on both sides before anything goes up. For a tension rod, check that it is not grinding paint off the jamb; a felt pad or a clean rubber end cap (if the rod has them) is cheaper than a scuffed frame. For adhesive hooks, stretch the tabs; do not pry them off with a screwdriver. For trim brackets, know that you are making small marks on purpose. Some renters decide that a pair of tiny holes in wood casing is easier to fill than drywall anchors. That is a judgment call, and it should be a conscious one, not a surprise in the last week of the lease.

If you later decide you need a true wraparound rod to kill the edge glow, that is usually a hardware conversation, not a no-drill one. Get it in writing if the landlord will allow brackets. Otherwise, stay with a tension or adhesive mount and improve the seal in other ways — fuller panels, a wrap if it can hang on the rod you already have, or the methods in the blackout guide.

Can I hang curtains in a rental with no holes?

Often, yes, if you pick a mount that matches the window. A tension rod inside a recessed frame is the usual no-holes starting point. Adhesive rod hooks and trim-tap brackets are the other two families, each with a different move-out trade-off.

Will a tension rod hold blackout curtains?

Sometimes, if the panels are not too heavy and the frame holds the spring. A tall, lined blackout pair can be a lot of weight. If the rod starts to walk down the jamb, the answer is no — go lighter, use two rods, or change the mount.

Do Kwik-Hang brackets damage anything?

They are designed to avoid drilling the wall by tapping into wood trim. That still leaves small marks in the casing. They are not a good fit for vinyl, foam, or crumbling trim, and they are not “no trace.”

Are wraparound blackout rods no-drill?

Usually not. The Amazon Basics wraparound rods we looked at are the right shape for reducing edge light, and they are typically screw-mounted. Treat them as a hardware option, not as a no-holes workaround.

What if the lease also bans adhesive?

Then a tension rod (which presses into the frame and does not use adhesive) or a written exception from the landlord is the path. Do not assume “no holes” automatically allows hooks and strips — read the clause.
